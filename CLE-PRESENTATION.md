# Defensible Documentation in Dementia Care
## Protecting the Fiduciary, the Client, and Your Practice

**A Continuing Legal Education Presentation**
**Draft script and course materials — Version 1.0**

## Presenter's preface (not part of the presentation)

This document is a script and outline for a one-hour (50–60 minute) CLE presentation aimed at elder law attorneys, estate planners, and elder law section members. It is designed to deliver genuine, accreditable legal education on documentation practices in dementia care, with a privacy-respecting documentation tool used as one worked illustration among general principles.

**Before presenting, you must:**

1. **Verify all legal content against your jurisdiction.** This script states general principles and uses placeholder citations marked `[CITE]`. You must insert and verify your state's statutes, rules, and case law. The author is not a lawyer; you are. Nothing here is legal advice.
2. **Secure CLE accreditation.** Requirements vary by state bar. Most require stated learning objectives, a minimum of substantive content, and — critically — restrictions on *commercial bias*. To remain accreditable, this presentation treats the documentation tool as one example of principles in practice, explicitly acknowledges alternative approaches, and never becomes a product demonstration. Keep it that way. If your state's rules are strict, present the tool segment as a neutral "evaluation framework" and let attendees draw their own conclusions.
3. **Disclose any interest.** If you have any relationship to the tool discussed, disclose it per your state's rules. Undisclosed commercial interest in an accredited CLE is an ethics problem in its own right.

This framing — education first, tool as illustration — is not only an accreditation requirement. It is the strategy. Attorneys recommend what they have been genuinely educated about, not what they have been sold.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this presentation, attendees will be able to:

1. **Identify** the four litigation and audit contexts in which contemporaneous care documentation is most often decisive: will contests, guardianship/conservatorship disputes, financial exploitation allegations, and Medicaid eligibility review.
2. **Articulate** the evidentiary difference between contemporaneous documentation and reconstructed testimony, and explain why the former protects both the fiduciary and the attorney who advised them.
3. **Counsel** clients on documentation practices for fiduciary decisions and capacity observation that will withstand later challenge.
4. **Evaluate** caregiving technology against the attorney's own duties of technological competence and client confidentiality.

## SEGMENT 1 — The Documentation Gap (≈10 minutes)

**[Open with the scenario.]**

Picture a client you have advised. An adult daughter holds a durable power of attorney for her mother, who has Alzheimer's disease. Over four years, the daughter makes hundreds of decisions: which medications to consent to, when to move her mother to memory care, how to spend down assets for Medicaid, whether to sell the family home. She acts in good faith and, by any reasonable measure, well.

Then her brother — absent for those four years — files a petition alleging she breached her fiduciary duty, exploited their mother financially, and acted against their mother's wishes.

Now ask the question that decides the case: **What can she prove?**

If the answer is "she remembers," she is in trouble. Memory is not evidence. Reconstructed testimony, offered years later by an interested party, is the weakest form of proof and the easiest to impeach. If the answer is "she has a contemporaneous record — dated, detailed, showing what she decided, why, what she knew of her mother's wishes, and whom she consulted" — the case looks entirely different.

**[The teaching point.]**

This is the documentation gap. Families in dementia care make consequential legal decisions continuously, under stress, with no idea they are building — or failing to build — the evidentiary record that will later determine whether they are vindicated or liable. And the attorneys who advised them are exposed alongside them: the first question in a fiduciary dispute is often *"What did your lawyer tell you to document?"*

The thesis of this hour: **In dementia care, the quality of contemporaneous documentation is frequently the difference between a defensible position and an indefensible one — for the fiduciary, for the client, and for you.** Counseling clients on documentation is not administrative housekeeping. It is core elder law risk management.

## SEGMENT 2 — Where Cases Are Won and Lost (≈15 minutes)

There are four recurring contexts where documentation is decisive. Walk through each.

### 2.1 Will and trust contests

The most common ground for contest is lack of testamentary capacity or undue influence `[CITE: state standard for testamentary capacity]`. When a person with a dementia diagnosis executes or amends an estate plan, the diagnosis itself becomes ammunition. The contestant argues: she had dementia, therefore she lacked capacity.

But a dementia diagnosis is not a capacity determination. Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific. A person with moderate dementia may retain testamentary capacity in a lucid interval `[CITE]`. What rebuts the "she had dementia" argument is **contemporaneous documentation of functional capacity around the relevant time** — observations of what she understood, how she communicated, whether she recognized the natural objects of her bounty.

Absent that, the proponent of the will is left arguing against a diagnosis with nothing but recollection.

### 2.2 Guardianship and conservatorship disputes

When a fiduciary's authority or conduct is challenged, the court asks whether the fiduciary acted within their authority and in the protected person's interest `[CITE: state fiduciary standard — best interest vs. substituted judgment]`. The fiduciary's defense is the record of their decision-making: what they decided, the basis for it, and its consistency with the principal's known wishes.

The standard in most jurisdictions favors **substituted judgment** where the principal's wishes are known — the fiduciary should do what the principal would have wanted, not merely what the fiduciary thinks best `[CITE]`. This makes documentation of the principal's *previously expressed wishes* extraordinarily valuable. A decision that looks questionable in isolation ("she moved her mother out of the family home") becomes defensible with a record ("mother stated in 2019, while competent, that she would prefer facility care to being a burden; three documented wandering incidents established the home was unsafe; her physician and her attorney were consulted").

### 2.3 Financial exploitation allegations

Elder financial exploitation is both a civil and criminal exposure, and family caregivers are frequently accused — sometimes rightly, often not `[CITE: state elder financial abuse statute]`. The accused caregiver who commingled funds, kept no receipts, and cannot account for expenditures looks guilty whether or not they are. The caregiver who documented every expense, its purpose, and its benefit to the principal has a defense.

The asymmetry is stark: the cost of documenting an expense is seconds; the cost of failing to document it, when accused, can be the case.

### 2.4 Medicaid eligibility and estate recovery

Medicaid's look-back period and the spend-down analysis turn on the *characterization* of transfers and expenditures `[CITE: 42 U.S.C. § 1396p; state Medicaid rules]`. A payment to a family caregiver may be a legitimate, compensable service or a disqualifying uncompensated transfer — and the difference often lies in whether there was a contemporaneous record establishing the care provided, its value, and the arrangement. After-the-fact reconstruction invites the agency to characterize transfers unfavorably. Contemporaneous documentation supports the favorable characterization.

**[Segment close.]**

Four contexts, one lesson: in each, the family's position is only as strong as what they recorded *at the time*. And in each, the attorney who advised them on documentation either protected them or left them exposed.

## SEGMENT 3 — What Defensible Documentation Looks Like (≈15 minutes)

If documentation is the protection, what makes documentation *defensible*? Six characteristics.

### 3.1 Contemporaneous

Created at or near the time of the event, not reconstructed later. Contemporaneous records carry far greater evidentiary weight and are far harder to impeach `[CITE: business records / present sense impression exceptions]`. A record made the day of a decision is evidence; a summary written for litigation is advocacy.

### 3.2 Specific and factual

Defensible documentation records observations, not conclusions. "Mother seemed fine" is worthless. "Mother knew the date, recognized her grandson, and explained why she wanted to change her will" is evidence. Counsel clients to record what they observed, not what they concluded.

### 3.3 Reasoned

For fiduciary decisions, the record should capture not just *what* was decided but *why* — the factors considered, the principal's known wishes, who was consulted. This is what demonstrates the fiduciary engaged in the deliberative process the law requires `[CITE]`.

### 3.4 Attributed and dated

Each entry should show who made it and when. A record that cannot be tied to an author and a date is easily challenged. Tamper-evidence — the ability to show a record was not altered after the fact — strengthens it further.

### 3.5 Preserved

Documentation that is lost is no documentation at all. Dementia care spans years; the record must survive device failures, the death of the principal, and the passage of time until the statute of limitations on any challenge has run.

### 3.6 Confidential

This is where many families err in the opposite direction — they document diligently in tools that expose the data. A shared cloud spreadsheet, a group chat, a consumer app that monetizes data: each creates its own exposure. The documentation must be protected, and protecting it implicates the attorney's own confidentiality obligations when the attorney recommends the method.

**[Teaching point.]**

Notice that these six characteristics — contemporaneous, specific, reasoned, attributed, preserved, confidential — are a *specification*. They are what you should counsel clients to achieve, regardless of method. A well-kept paper journal can satisfy the first four. The last two — preservation across years and genuine confidentiality — are where method matters most, and where technology becomes relevant.

## SEGMENT 4 — The Technology Question (≈10 minutes)

**[Begin with the attorney's own duty.]**

Most states have adopted some form of the duty of technological competence — the obligation to understand the benefits and risks of relevant technology `[CITE: ABA Model Rule 1.1, Comment 8; your state's adoption]`. When you advise an elder law client, the technology they use to document care is now within the scope of your competent counsel. And Rule 1.6's confidentiality duty `[CITE]` means that if you recommend a tool, you should understand whether that tool protects or exposes your client's information.

So how should an attorney evaluate a caregiving documentation tool? Not by its features, but by a small set of questions that map directly to the risks we have discussed. These questions are method-neutral — they apply equally to apps, spreadsheets, and paper:

1. **Where does the data live, and who else can read it?** A tool that stores your client's medical, financial, and legal information on a vendor's servers has created a new locus of exposure — to breach, to subpoena, to the vendor's own use. Local-first tools, which keep data on the client's own device, minimize this.

2. **Is the data encrypted, and is the vendor able to read it?** Encryption at rest means a lost device is not a disclosure. A vendor who cannot read the data cannot be compelled to produce it or tempted to use it.

3. **Does the tool surveil the user?** Analytics and telemetry about a dementia patient's care are themselves sensitive. A tool that collects usage data has created a record of your client's behavior that they did not intend.

4. **Can the client get their data out, and does it survive the vendor?** Dementia care spans years. A tool that locks data in a proprietary format or disappears with the vendor fails the preservation requirement.

5. **Does the tool support attribution and tamper-evidence?** For fiduciary documentation specifically, the ability to show who recorded what, when, and that it was not later altered, directly serves the defensibility we discussed.

6. **How is the tool paid for?** A tool funded by monetizing user data has an interest adverse to your client's confidentiality.

**[The illustration — kept neutral and brief.]**

To make this concrete, consider how these questions apply to one category of tool: privacy-first, local-first caregiving applications. As one worked example, [a tool I will reference generically, or name with disclosure] is built to store care data encrypted on the family's own device, with no vendor server, no analytics, role-based access so a hired aide sees care information but not the family's finances, structured logs for fiduciary decisions and capacity observations with attribution and timestamps, and full data export. Whether or not you recommend this particular tool, it illustrates that the six characteristics of defensible documentation *can* be met in software — and gives you a benchmark against which to evaluate any tool a client brings to you.

The broader point is not the tool. It is that you now have a framework. When a client asks "should I use this app to track Mom's care?" you can answer as a competent elder law attorney rather than deferring.

## SEGMENT 5 — Practical Implementation (≈8 minutes)

How to operationalize this in your practice:

### At the engagement

When you establish a power of attorney, guardianship, or care arrangement, **counsel documentation from day one.** The decisions that get challenged are often the early ones, made before anyone thought to keep records. Build a documentation conversation into your intake.

### A documentation checklist for clients

Give fiduciary clients a simple specification — the six characteristics — and concrete guidance:
- Record decisions when you make them, not later.
- For each significant decision: what you decided, why, what you knew of the principal's wishes, whom you consulted, and the outcome.
- Record observations of the principal's functioning factually and regularly, especially around any estate planning or major decision.
- Keep expense records contemporaneously, with purpose and benefit noted.
- Use a method that preserves the record for years and keeps it confidential.

### The capacity observation practice

For clients facing likely capacity challenges, counsel a regular, structured record of the principal's functional abilities over time — not a diagnosis, but lay observations of what the person can and cannot do, dated and attributed. This longitudinal record is often more persuasive to a court than a single physician's letter, because it shows the trajectory and is harder to dismiss as a snapshot `[CITE]`.

### Your own file

Document *your* counsel. When you advise a client to keep records, note that you did so. The attorney who can show they counseled proper documentation is protected even if the client failed to follow through.

## Closing (≈2 minutes)

Return to the daughter from the opening. The difference between her vindication and her liability was never her good faith — she had that throughout. The difference was whether four years of good-faith decisions left a record capable of proving themselves.

Our clients cannot un-make the decisions of dementia care. But we can ensure they make them on the record — contemporaneously, specifically, with reasons, attributed, preserved, and confidential. That record protects the fiduciary who acted well, the principal whose wishes are honored and proven, and the attorney whose counsel is demonstrated.

Documentation is not the unglamorous part of elder law. In the contexts that actually reach litigation, it is frequently the whole case.

**[End. Move to Q&A.]**
